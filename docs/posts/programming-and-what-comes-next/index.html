And now that you don't have to be perfect, you can be good

A subjective view of my career as a programmer and what comes next.

April 30, 2026

The title is taken from John Steinbeck, East of Eden.

The first day I worked with a computer, I knew I could sit behind it for the rest of my life. Now, in the world as it is, I no longer hesitate to admit this. Maybe I once held back out of fear of being seen as a geek or just a little odd, which I am. But it was always in me. I just liked it, with all the things it offered at the time (around 2002) and all the things I thought I could do with it in the future.

Software was magic for me, and I was interested in learning more about it to such an extent that, in the years ahead, I, as a kid from a small, overlooked corner of the world, far from the languages and cultures that shaped computing, somehow taught myself enough broken English to understand it better. It was an unexplainable, effortless passion. I don’t know whether it is universal for people to discover, as kids, something they care about that deeply, and when talking about childhood, it is hard to know whether memories are real or just another fabrication the subconscious has slipped in along the way. But now, looking back, I am convinced that if you could truly look at the heart of things, there was some unusual obsession with computers in me.

Having found that passion, after a short period of doubt about continuing or abandoning it, I stuck with it. I got a related master’s degree and built my career around programming. It didn’t work out greatly, but it worked in some way. I have a salary in the field, and I have enjoyed working in it most of the time.

In every career there are different spectrums of talent; programming is no different. At one end, there are legends like Dennis Ritchie, Linus Torvalds, and John Carmack, who wrote software that changed the world and are known by millions. At the other end of that spectrum, as I slowly realized later in life, are people like me. But programming has this unique feature that you can make a life doing it without being too good at it. And I know this is a rarity in the world. Most people don’t enjoy their jobs, and knowing that, I accepted my place in the spectrum, and honestly, I am still happy and grateful for this opportunity.

AI was not a totally unknown surprise here. If you worked or studied software, you knew about AI. Actually, my first real job was working with dialogue state graphs and text classifiers to create something similar to IBM Watson. It was there from the early days of software, progressing slowly with occasional leaps. The goal of solving natural language understanding and creating human-like agents was also always there, and why shouldn’t it be? After all, God created humanity in His own image, reflecting His likeness, and from the first day it was only natural for us to create something reflecting our likeness in machines. It was always one of the goals, if not the most important one. It was there, standing on a distant horizon, but I and the people around me realistically never thought we would deal with such things within our lifetime.

Things happened faster than expected. Transformer architectures changed the pace of progress by making sequence modeling easier to scale and easier to train, and after that, as it turned out, attention was all we needed, along with reinforcement learning and instruction tuning to understand natural language. I know it required the effort of many people and the shoulders of giants, but for the sake of romanticizing it, let’s say it was that simple.

Surprisingly or not, there wasn’t too much difference between understanding natural language and machine language, at least for the kind of code we usually write. And for people like me, who have software as both a career and a main hobby, whether we admit it or not, it started to feel scarier and scarier by the day. The question started to pop up in our minds:

“Should I be a carpenter?”1 #

For a long time, I tried to solve this problem. I read AI accelerationist opinions, I read the opposite view, I read why AI was just a passing hype, I thought about which branches of programming are riskier, I read about who will survive, and so on. But deep down I also know it is not something that can really be argued away. Whatever is going to turn out to be true, and whatever we believe, I think we can agree that things are changing in this field in ways that none of us can truly predict.

So about this essay: it isn’t an objective or technical argument. After all, that is all I have always done, or tried to do. For a long time I thought about starting to write technical posts on my blog to keep myself active, and I procrastinated on it for eternity. Today I was talking to one of my friends, and he was considering changing his career. And it triggered this dread in me. I thought I had a solid opinion about this to put out there. I came here to persuade all of you, and mainly myself, that this is the golden era of programming.

But the more I wrote about it, the less it felt like a clear conclusion. So ironically, I turned to AI to help me finish this essay. It gave me several suggestions for endings, all of them clean, well-structured, and probably correct in their own way. But the problem was that they felt too complete, like they resolved something I haven’t resolved yet.

Now that I’m thinking about it more, I have less of a solid argument and more of a still-living passion for software in my heart. After all, it’s both my career and my main hobby. I still enjoy solving hard programming problems late into the night, and I can’t imagine myself not liking it.

I leave it here, at this point, still thinking about it, without a proper ending.

  1. I borrowed “Should I be a carpenter?” from the title of the first episode of Wading Through AI podcast series. Unlike me, Demetri and Casey are people who actually know what they are talking about, and I would highly suggest checking it out if you want a more professional discussion on this subject. I could literally listen to them talk about almost anything for hours. ↩︎
